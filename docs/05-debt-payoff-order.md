# Debt payoff order that respects math and morale

*Reading time: about 10 minutes. This is chapter 05 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

There are two famous theories about the best order to pay off debts. One is pure math. One is pure psychology. Both are correct, which is annoying — and also helpful, because it means you're allowed to blend them. This chapter shows you the math version, the morale version, and a hybrid that most actual humans end up using.

You'll want the [debt payoff worksheet](worksheet-debt-payoff-plan.md) open: one table, every debt listed once, ordered your way.

## Ground rule zero: minimums paid is the first win

Before arguing avalanche-vs-snowball, protect the foundation: **every account gets its minimum payment, every month, on time.** Missed minimums don't just add late fees — they can trigger penalty interest rates that make every other number in this chapter worse. Automate the minimums (the tip from [chapter 04](04-how-scores-work.md)), then treat "extra payoff money" as a question of where, not whether.

## The math version: avalanche

Pay minimums everywhere, and put every spare dollar at the **highest interest rate first** — usually a card at 20-something percent APR, while a car loan at 5% waits in line. When the top debt is paid, its old minimum *joins* the attack fund (the "rolling minimum" effect), which is what makes the snowball-owning avalanche faster than it looks.

Why the ranking matters so much: interest at 24% APR means roughly **a fifth of the balance, every year, added back**. A $3,000 card at a typical card APR works off about $55 of interest in a single month — interest that a payment plan aimed anywhere else pays first.

**Honest strength:** mathematically fastest, cheapest, cleanest. **Honest weakness:** if your highest-rate debt is also your *largest* — say a $9,000 card over three smaller ones — it can take many months before you see a single account vanish. Plenty of good plans die of unreachable progress bars.

## The morale version: snowball

Pay minimums everywhere, and put every spare dollar at the **smallest balance first** — regardless of rate. Each debt *closed* is an account that no longer exists: one fewer bill, one fewer due date, one fewer row. It usually isn't the math-optimal order, typically costing modestly more in interest, but the visible wins arrive early and arrive *repeatedly*.

**Honest strength:** real, sustained motivation; the "I actually stuck with it" factor is worth more than 0.5% interest optimization if the alternative is stopping in month two. **Honest weakness:** it pays more interest than necessary — so if you can get your wins elsewhere, the pure version leaves money on the table.

## The hybrid most real plans use

1. **Automate minimums everywhere** (ground rule zero — non-negotiable).
2. **Clear one genuinely quick win first.** A paid-off $180 balance in week two buys morale you'll spend months. If your smallest debt is under a month's spare cash, take it first regardless of rate.
3. **Then go after highest-rate debt,** avalanche-style, until either the plan finishes or morale *visibly* sags.
4. **If morale sags,** snap back to a small-balance win — the fastest possible closure to point at — then return to avalanche.
5. Keep the list on one page (that's the worksheet) and cross off rows in ink.

You are not in a confessional: paying off a small debt "for morale" that math purists would decline is still, unambiguously, a debt paid off.

## The math, one small worked example

Suppose three debts: A) a card, $1,200 at 24% APR, minimum $35; B) a card, $4,500 at 19% APR, minimum $80; C) a personal loan, $1,500 at 9% APR, minimum $60. You have $260 a month total for debt; the minimums take $175, leaving $85 of extra firepower.

- **Avalanche order:** A (24%), then B (19%), then C (9%). Least interest paid overall — the maximum-math answer.
- **Snowball order:** A (smallest balance), then C ($1,500), then B ($4,500). Every balance-rank closed is an account off the list.

Notice something useful: **the first target is the same in both theories.** The plans only differ at the second and third stops. Most people's lists overlap more than the internet debate suggests — which is why *where you put the $85* matters far more than which name you pick for it.

## Building the "attack fund," the calm way

- **Label it.** A separate savings pocket — even a labeled envelope of cash — that exists only for debt payoff. Designated money behaves differently than leftover money.- **Route every extra dollar to exactly one target.** The current worksheet row is the only address for bonus paychecks, sold stuff, and canceled subscriptions. (A canceled recurring charge is a permanent monthly raise; the companion guide *subscription-audit-handbook* walks through finding them.)
- **Keep a small starter cushion.** A few hundred dollars stashed somewhere calm — not because saving beats paying, but because a plan with zero buffer gets repriced into new balances by the first flat tire. The cushion protects the plan; the plan outgrows it.
- **Schedule the extra on payday, not "what's left at month's end."** Leftover money at month's end is usually a rumor. The worksheet has a place to write the payday, which remarkably few plans ever fill in.

## Consolidation, and other tools with fine print

- **A consolidation loan or balance transfer can genuinely help** — if the *total interest over the loan* comes out lower than the current path **and** the emptied cards stay empty afterward. A lower monthly payment with a much longer term can easily mean *more* total interest; do that division before approving anything, and give the Freed card a boring job if you keep it open.
- **Avoid anything involving "quick credit" or rotating balances as a lifestyle.** There is no version of that which ends well; chapter [07](07-spotting-credit-and-debt-scams.md) names the specific flavors.

## The kinds of debt that follow different rules, briefly

- **Student loans:** separate system — income-driven repayment plans, consolidation, and forgiveness-specific rules, all documented through the loan servicer and the Department of Education. Paying a federal student loan down like a credit card is often leaving better options untouched; read the options first.
- **Medical bills:** ask about the provider's financial assistance policy before paying anything past minimums — many hospitals *have* one, and by law they must generally offer it before aggressive collection. Many medical bills also negotiate *far* better than any card.
- **Taxes and court debts:** distinct rules and deadlines (and enforcement powers). Prioritize by the *consequences*: a tax levy is not like a card late, and the IRS and your state levy-specific systems have their own rules and (some) hardship pauses.

## When you can't make the minimums at all

That's a different starting line, and it has real resources:

- **Nonprofit credit counseling.** Agencies affiliated with genuine nonprofits (the National Foundation for Credit Counseling network is the classic example) offer real counseling — budgeting help, negotiation of hardship plans, and debt-management setup — for free or modest fees. Verify the nonprofit-ness before agreeing to anything ([chapter 07](07-spotting-credit-and-debt-scams.md) has the checklist); many "credit counseling" ads are debt-settlement mills in costume.
- **Call the creditor's hardship line *first*.** Issuers have internal hardship programs (rate reductions, skipping a month) that they generally advertise quietly. No fee. Company policy expires; asking costs a phone call.
- The collection-call scripts in [chapter 06](06-collector-call-scripts.md) then belong in your glovebox.

A steady pace beats a heroic one: minimums automated, one worksheet, one target at a time, crossed off in ink. Ten months later the list is a little shorter, and the person holding it is measurably lighter.
