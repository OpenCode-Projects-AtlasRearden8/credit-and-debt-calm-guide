# Freezes beat monitoring

*Reading time: about 8 minutes. This is chapter 03 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

This is the shortest argument in the whole guide:

**Monitoring tells you after someone has already opened a new account in your name. A freeze stops them from opening it in the first place.**

One is a doorbell. One is a lock. Both are free under federal law, and the lock costs a few minutes of your evening.

## The honest comparison

| Question | Credit freeze | Monitoring (paid or free) |
|---|---|---|
| Prevents new fraudulent accounts? | **Yes** — nothing can be opened while frozen | No — it only *reports* after the fact |
| Notification speed | n/a (nothing to notify) | Days after the fraud effectively started |
| Costs money | **No** — legally free at each bureau | Paid plans exist; often bundled with upsells |
| Affects your credit score | **No** | No |
| Can you still use your own cards? | **Yes** — freezes block *new* accounts, not existing ones | Yes |
| Harmless to forget about | **Yes** — a freeze stays indefinitely until you lift it | Subscriptions lapse and stop protecting you |
| Can be "scammed" out of you? | Nothing to sell, nothing to steal | Somebody talking you into a "free trial" that quietly renews |

Monitoring isn't useless. It fits a narrow set of situations, listed at the end. But as a *default* for everyday people, the freeze wins decisively — and the monitoring industry's ads are, unsurprisingly, quiet about that. A service that can't undo a mistake in progress is a **notification**, not protection.

## What a freeze actually is

A security credit freeze tells each credit bureau: *no one — including a scammer posing as you — can pull a new credit file from you unless you say so with a special lift.* Since an application for a new card or loan requires the lender to pull your file, a frozen file blocks new-account fraud at the source, cold.

Freezes place a few uncommon worries to rest immediately:

- **It doesn't affect your credit score.** A frozen file still reports, ages, and is fed to your existing accounts. Nothing about the freeze touches scoring.
- **It doesn't hurt your relationship with cards and loans you already have.** Payments, rewards, and limit increases you request through an existing account: all still work.
- **It's free by federal law**, at each of the three nationwide bureaus, and you can lift or temporarily lift it as often as you like — a freeze only exists until you say otherwise.

## How to freeze at each bureau

You'll freeze at each of the three — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — because lenders pull from different bureaus, and a freeze at one doesn't stop anything at the others. Each bureau's website has a "freeze" or "security freeze" section; each takes a few minutes, requires identity verification, and ends with a confirmation (and sometimes a PIN — keep those in your password manager or on paper somewhere *finding-able*).

Practical guidance that holds for all three:

1. **Freeze online, on each bureau's own site** (navigate from your browser, not links in email — typosquatting exists here too, as everywhere).
2. **Confirm the freeze is active** — a confirmation screen, an account within the bureau's dashboard, a written notice. Save it.
3. **Decide your policy on future credit**: most people put a note in their calendar for "before applying for a loan, thaw at whichever bureau my lender uses, usually online and ready within an hour" (chapter [04](04-how-scores-work.md) notes that lenders usually pull one bureau, but not always).
4. **Optional, extra-credit:** freeze the *fourth* less-known bureau, [Innovis](https://www.innovis.com), and other specialty-reporting shops (like the bank-account screeners). It's the same argument as freezing all three: the system is only as locked as its least-locked file.

Also worth doing, because they're free and take minutes: **freeze your children's and (with consent, if applicable) your straightforward elderly relatives' credit.** Minors rarely have files at all — creating a protected one, rather than discovering one later, is one of those kindnesses grown-up wizards regret not doing sooner.

## Thawing on purpose (the only real "inconvenience")

The freeze has exactly one logistical wrinkle: when *you* want new credit, the file has to be open to the lender. The workflow is:

1. Know roughly when you're applying (a loan pre-screen, an apartment, a card signup).
2. **Unfreeze — permanently or temporarily — at the bureau or bureaus the lender will pull.** Temporary "one-week thaw windows" exist at each bureau's portal and are the kindest default: you unfreeze for a set window, and it goes back to frozen by itself after.
3. Apply. Everything else about the application proceeds as normal.

People work themselves into a fright about thaw timing. In practice portals handle same-day and scheduled thaws easily, and the worst case is you unfreeze one bureau, wait an afternoon, and try again.

## Freeze vs. lock vs. fraud alert (quick decrypt)

- **Credit freeze** (sometimes "security freeze"): the *statutory* tool. Free, federal-law-backed, doesn't lapse if you forget it exists.
- **"Lock" products** (with swipe-one-finger apps): a contractual product the bureaus sell *alongside* the freeze. Convenient, and fine to use *in addition*, but it's a promise by a company rather than a right of yours. If given a choice between paying $29.99/mo for a lock or using the free freeze — the freeze wins for the same reason the door does.
- **Fraud alert**: a flag lenders should take extra steps before extending credit. A basic alert lasts a year (there are longer ones for active-duty and identity-theft victims), is placed *at one bureau and referred to the other two*, and works *alongside* a freeze nicely — in fact, if you're already an identity-theft victim, place a fraud alert **and** use the freeze, since they protect different things (alert = behavior of lenders *before* opening; freeze = whether a request can even be processed).

## When monitoring still earns its keep

Keeping the promise from before: monitoring has real uses, just narrower ones than the infomercial version:

- **After a confirmed identity-theft incident**, while you're cleaning up: eyes-on across bureaus is reassuring in the recovery window.
- **You're rebuilding credit actively** and want to *see* changes to reports and scores weekly (also genuinely useful, also free at several bureaus' basic tiers).
- **Someone else is already paying for it** (a benefit bundled by your bank or employer): take it — free is free; just don't stack a paid product under one you already have.

If none of those fit, the calm path is the same four moves: pull reports weekly-ish ([chapter 01](01-pull-your-reports-safely.md)), dispute errors in letters ([chapter 02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md)), **freeze**, and don't send anyone a monthly subscription fee to do what a locked door does better.
