# Spotting credit-repair and debt-settlement scams

*Reading time: about 9 minutes. This is chapter 07 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

This is a harder chapter to write than the others, because the scams in it aren't shady-alleys-with-fake-mustaches. Most of them are companies with professional websites, reassuring phone numbers, and (sometimes) real-looking non-profit names. The tell is never the design. The tell is **what they promise and what they charge, and the order those arrive in.**

Here's the industry-shaped fact this whole guide leans on: **everything legal that credit repair can do, you can do yourself, for free** — pull reports ([01](01-pull-your-reports-safely.md)), dispute in letters ([02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md)), freeze ([03](03-freezes-beat-monitoring.md)), and pay in a chosen order ([05](05-debt-payoff-order.md)). Any company charging hundreds of dollars "to fix your credit" is charging you for tasks you own outright. A few do the tasks. Many just talk. Some simply take the money.

## The legal right that makes the scam game obvious

Two federal laws set the floor, and knowing them reframes every pitch:

- **Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA):** repair companies must give you a written contract spelling out exactly what they'll do, plus a three-day cancellation right — and, the big one, **they may not collect any fee until after the promised results are actually delivered.** A company asking $300 up front is breaking the law or relabeling the fee as a "processing charge." Either way: walk.
- **No upfront fees for debt settlement:** settlement companies generally can't collect their fees until a settlement is actually reached and agreed to, and your payment schedule must be transparent. (Some law-firm-affiliated outfits structure fees differently under state rules — treat any "attorney model" with an upfront retainer as exactly the kind of thing to scrutinize twice.) Shopping reflex for both industries alike: **no effect, no fees.**

## Red flags, translated

The phrases below are the actual sentences that come out of this industry. When you hear one, stop listening and start walking:

- "**We can remove accurate negative information.**" Nobody can, legally. Disputes remove *inaccurate* information (chapter [02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md)). A company promising otherwise is selling deletion of the truth — which makes it either a lie or part of a scheme, and not your problem.
- "**Ninety days to a higher score — guaranteed.**" No legitimate company guarantees score outcomes, because scores are formulas run on *your* data over *time*, not switches they click. Anyone who says "guaranteed" in the first conversation is telling you the rest of the pitch directly.
- "**A new credit identity — legally!**" This is the CPN scam: "CPN" means a *Credit Privacy Number*, which is a real thing that exists only in certain lawful government contexts; it is *not* a legal substitute for your SSN on loan applications. Using a fake identity number on a credit application is a serious federal offense, and the seller is not the one who gets prosecuted.
- "**We dispute everything — that's how it works.**" Template-mill disputes (challenging accurate and inaccurate items alike, often via reused loop phrases) can be declined as frivolous, and a pattern of them can make future legitimate disputes harder. This costs you months, not just money.
- "**Don't contact the bureaus yourself — let us handle everything.**" You always have the right to correspond with the bureaus and creditors directly. Routing every dispute through someone else hands them your paper trail — and paper trail is what makes a [CFPB complaint](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/) work when things go sideways.
- "**Just pay us (not your creditors) monthly, and we'll settle for pennies.**" That's the debt-settlement *model*: you stop paying your creditors, your late fees and credit damage compound while the settle-fund grows, then they negotiate. It *sometimes* produces a settlement — after serious credit damage, on settlement terms with genuine bankruptcy territory, and honestly priced. Which brings us to...

## Debts to be settled: the honest version

Debt settlement (negotiating payment of less than you owe, usually in a lump sum) is **not always a scam.** It's a tool with a real price tag, and the honest version *says so*:

- **Credit damage is dramatic and immediate** — accounts stop being paid, go late, and can head to collection while the settlement fund grows. That damage is *part of the product,* not a side effect you can manage away.
- **Forgiven debt is generally taxable income.** The forgiven difference is generally reported (the 1099-C form exists for exactly this), with some exceptions — like documented insolvency — worth understanding *before* you're holding the form at tax time rather than after. A one-hour chat with a tax pro before a big settlement is cheap by any standard.
- **Creditors can sue before they'll settle.** No settlement company controls another company's lawyers; some of the mail during the waiting period comes from actual attorneys, sometimes with court dates.
- Paying the *full* balance through a debt-management plan at a nonprofit counselor (chapter [05](05-debt-payoff-order.md)) usually beats paying less than owed via settlement — less drama, less tax surprise, less credit carve-up — which is why the honest version of settlement narrows: for many people, it's the plan you choose *when the other plans can't keep up.*

**Red flags for settlement companies specifically:**

- Fees collected monthly, up front, before any settlement has happened.
- "We can guarantee to stop creditor calls" (chapter [06](06-collector-call-scripts.md) contains the honest method, free).
- "Get out of debt fast — 12 months!" (Real settlement timelines rarely run that short; promises this quick are usually the math being invented.)
- Encouraging you to stop communicating with your creditors without an exit plan.
- "Don't bother talking to a bankruptcy attorney — we handle everything." (Bankruptcy attorneys sometimes sell plans that make settlement companies unnecessary; their advice costs you nothing to hear, which is exactly why a settlement company doesn't want you to.)

## Quick vetting routine for any company in the money-crunch space

1. **Ask for the contract scope *before* paying anything, and read the fee schedule** — including when fees are due.
2. **Check regulatory honesty:** search the company name on the [CFPB complaint database](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints/) and with your **state Attorney General's consumer-protection site** (search "company name + state AG").
3. **Verify "nonprofit" really is.** "Nonprofit" is easy to claim and easy to check. Ask for — and read — their tax-exempt determination letter, and default to counselors affiliated with recognized networks like the National Foundation for Credit Counseling instead of trusting a website's word choice.
4. **Search the promise, not the company:** type the *exact claim* you were quoted (e.g., "CPN legally," "guaranteed score boost") plus "FTC" into a search box. The FTC's plain-English pages on these exact pitches give you a coherent second opinion in minutes.
5. **Sleep on it.** Legitimate services survive a 48-hour cooling-off; sway tactics don't. If someone tells you the "special pricing expires today," *that's the tell about the offer* — not about your decision-making.
