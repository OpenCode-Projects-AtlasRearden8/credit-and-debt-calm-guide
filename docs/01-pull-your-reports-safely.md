# Pull your own reports safely

*Reading time: about 8 minutes. This is chapter 01 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

You can't straighten a shelf you can't see. Credit repair — the real, legal, free kind — always starts the same way: you pull your own reports, you read them carefully, and you write down what's wrong. This chapter is about doing that safely, without giving your personal information to a lookalike website or, somehow, dinging your own score.

## First, the reassurance: pulling your own report never hurts your score

There are two kinds of lookups on your credit file:

- A **hard inquiry** happens when a *lender* checks your report because you applied for credit. Hard inquiries can shave a few points off for a while.
- A **soft inquiry** is anything else: you checking your own file, a card issuer checking whether to send you an offer, an employer you consented to, a landlord. Soft inquiries are invisible to lenders and never touch your score.

Checking your own report is a soft inquiry. Always. Pull it every week if you like — the score doesn't notice and, more importantly, neither does anyone else.

## The one place to get the official free reports

The place is **[annualcreditreport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com)** — the site the three nationwide bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) are required to participate in by federal law. Reports there are free, and as of recent years they're free **once a week from each bureau**.

A few plain-English cautions:

- Beware lookalikes. Ads and "check your score free!" links often route you to paid errands-in-disguise (score subscriptions, "premium" report bundles). If a site asks for a card number to show you a "free" report, that is not the official site.
- The bureaus' own websites also sell copies and wrap them in upsell flows. The official free route is annualcreditreport.com; the bureaus' sites matter later, for [freezing](03-freezes-beat-monitoring.md).
- The form will ask for your Social Security number, date of birth, and identity-verification questions. That is normal *on the official site* — it is precisely why you should never end up on a copycat.

## What a credit report actually contains

A report is less mysterious than its reputation. It has four main sections:

1. **Personal information.** Name, addresses, Social Security number (usually masked), sometimes employment. Errors are common and mostly harmless — except old addresses can hint that accounts were opened in your name long ago. Fix obvious errors; the bureaus don't score this section.
2. **Accounts (tradelines).** Every account — cards, loans, lines — with the open date, credit limit or original loan amount, balance, payment history month by month, and a status (current, late, charged off, in collection, etc.). This is where the reading happens. Errors hide here.
3. **Inquiries.** Hard inquiries (applications, visible to lenders) and soft inquiries (everything else). Expect a handful of soft inquiries you never initiated; that's the system offering you things.
4. **Collections and public records.** Debts sent to collection agencies, and certain court-related items. Obsolete items — most negative information is supposed to drop off after about 7 years (bankruptcies, about 10) — sometimes linger wrongly. That's a dispute candidate.

## The line-by-line reading habit

Set aside 30 unhurried minutes per report. For each account row, ask five questions:

- [ ] **Is this mine?** An account you don't recognize is not a "probably fine." It needs a look: identity theft moves first through unfamiliar tradelines.
- [ ] **Are the numbers right?** Balances and limits drift; the *reported* limit matters a lot for utilization (see [chapter 04](04-how-scores-work.md)). A limit reported as much lower than it really is can unfairly sink a score.
- [ ] **Is the status right?** Late marks, charge-offs, and collections have outsized impact. A single 30-day late that never happened is worth disputing even though it's small.
- [ ] **Is the date right?** Check the "reported" and "first delinquency" style dates. Shaving a year off when a debt *should* fall off is one of the classic reporting bugs.
- [ ] **Does it appear twice?** The same debt sold between collectors sometimes shows up twice, from two different agencies. Duplicates are disputes, plain and simple.

Keep a written list as you go. Chapter [02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md) turns each line item into a short, factual dispute, and the [dispute tracker worksheet](worksheet-dispute-tracker.md) keeps the whole campaign on one page.

## Store your copies locally, and keep them yours

This guide is local-first, so download the PDFs rather than trusting screenshots in someone's app:

- Create a folder like `credit-reports/2026-10/` with one file per bureau.
- Save any password-protected ZIPs with a password you'll remember.
- If you ever share a report (a dispute letter enclosure, a mortgage officer, a renter application), **redact your Social Security number** from the copy you send. A black marker on paper, or a white shape in any PDF viewer, takes ten seconds.
- Never email a full report to a stranger, a "credit repair company," or an online form. Chapter [07](07-spotting-credit-and-debt-scams.md) exists because such requests are how theft starts wearing a helpful costume.

## Rhythm: how often should you pull?

A calm cadence beats obsessive checking:

- **Steady state:** one bureau a week on a rotating basis (weeks cycle through all three, monthly). Total time: about ten minutes.
- **Before a big application** (mortgage, car loan): pull *all three* at least a month ahead, so disputes have time to resolve.
- **After a suspicious event** (lost wallet, odd email about an account): pull first, then read [chapter 03](03-freezes-beat-monitoring.md) about freezing so the shutoff valve is already closed.

## If you can't get your reports online

Identity verification sometimes fails (moves, thin files, name changes). Two calm fallbacks:

- Request by mail using the form available via annualcreditreport.com / the CFPB's instructions, with copies (never originals) of identity documents.
- Wait a week and retry online — the verification questions are drawn from data-like pools and sometimes refresh in your favor.

## What to do with what you find

- **Correct stuff, wrong numbers, unknown names, stale items:** that's chapter [02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md) territory.
- **An account you genuinely never opened:** that's identity theft. Also chapter [02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md), plus a police report *plus* the FTC's [identitytheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov) — then freeze before you dispute, so it can't happen again while you clean up.
- **Accurate but painful stuff:** accurate negative information can't be deleted by anyone, legally. What you *can* do is make sure it's *correctly* dated and reported, and start the new habits in chapters [04](04-how-scores-work.md) and [05](05-debt-payoff-order.md) that outrun it with time.

You now own everything "credit repair" actually is: your reports, on your screen, read carefully, in your folder. The rest of this guide just makes the moving parts move.
