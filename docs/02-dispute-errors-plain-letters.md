# Dispute errors in plain letters

*Reading time: about 9 minutes. This is chapter 02 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

You found something wrong in your report. Here's the good news, and it's better news than the credit-repair industry would like you to hear: **you can fix most report errors yourself, in a letter shorter than a postcard, for free.** This chapter shows you how — and why the calm, plain letter is more effective than the ten-page legal-sounding tantrum.

## What actually counts as a dispute-able error

Disputes work for *inaccurate or outdated* information:

- An account that isn't yours.
- A wrong balance, credit limit, or open date.
- A late payment, charge-off, or collection that didn't happen or belongs to a different account.
- The same debt reported twice (classic: a collection that was sold from agency to agency appears twice).
- Old information that should have fallen off (most negatives age off after about 7 years; bankruptcies after about 10).
- Statuses that don't match reality — "charged off" when the account was paid as agreed, or a paid-in-full loan still showing a balance.

Disputes do **not** work — for anyone, legally — on accurate information you'd simply rather have deleted. That's important: the promise "we remove accurate negatives!" is the #1 flag in [chapter 07](07-spotting-credit-and-debt-scams.md). What you *can* still do about accurate-but-painful history is make sure it's reported *correctly and completely*, and outrun it (chapters [04](04-how-scores-work.md) and [05](05-debt-payoff-order.md)).

## Your right, in one paragraph

Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), the credit bureaus are generally required to investigate a dispute within about **30 days** of receiving it (occasionally 45, in specific situations), then tell you the result and correct or delete anything they can't verify. The business or "data furnisher" that reported the item also has obligations. You'll see this echoed chapter-wide: disputes are a *right*, not a favor.

If a bureau's reply is a bare "verified as accurate" with no real investigation — and that happens — escalate: dispute with more evidence, then file a complaint with the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/) and your state Attorney General. Complaint filing is free and creates a paper trail.

## Online dispute or mail dispute?

Both work. They're different shapes:

| | Online | Mail (certified) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Faster turnaround | Weeks |
| Paper trail | Portal screenshots | Certified-mail receipt + return receipt |
| Detail room | Character-limited forms | As much as you need, with enclosures |
| Best for | Simple, single-item errors | Multi-item disputes, anything with documents |

For one easy fix (a typo'd balance), online is fine. For anything with more than one wrong row, or anything where you want proof of what you said and when — mail it, certified with return receipt. The [letter templates](dispute-letter-templates.md) are built for that route, and the [dispute tracker worksheet](worksheet-dispute-tracker.md) starts its clock when the letter leaves your hand.

> **One suspicion worth respecting:** if the error looks like identity theft rather than a data slip — an account you never opened — freeze your credit ([chapter 03](03-freezes-beat-monitoring.md)) *first*, then file at [identitytheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov) and dispute with that report attached. Order matters here.

## Why a plain letter beats an angry one

The person opening your mail at the bureau is not your enemy and has about 30 seconds to categorize your dispute. Short, factual letters win because:

- **Each fact is checkable.** "Preferred reporting agency: TransUnion's own trade line for account #... shows limit of $500; statement shows $4,000. Enclosed." No thunder required.
- **The ask is unambiguous.** "Please correct the limit" or "please delete the duplicate" — one specific action each.
- **Calm reads as credible.** Screaming, ALL-CAPS PHRASES, and legal threats a letter-writer clearly copies from the internet do the opposite of what people intuit.

Nobody is being polite at the expense of firmness here. Firm is: short sentences, precise facts, one ask per item, deadline awareness. Warm is the *absence* of flailing around them.

## The anatomy of an effective dispute letter

1. **Header:** your name, address, date. Reference any bureau-supplied report number if you have one.
2. **One short opening line:** what you're doing ("I am disputing the following items in my credit file").
3. **Itemized errors:** one numbered block per item — bureau, account identifier, what's wrong, *what's right instead* (this half is the one people forget and it is what makes the letter actionable), and the specific correction you're asking for.
4. **Enclosures:** copies (never originals) of proof — a bill, a statement, a police or FTC identity-theft report, a court disposition. And a copy of your ID plus a recent utility bill or bank statement to prove you're you.
5. **One closing line:** "Please investigate and correct these items within the statutory period, and notify me of the results in writing."
6. **Sign it** in ink, keep a copy of everything sent, and leave the return receipt in the folder until the outcome lands.

Full fill-in versions live in the [templates chapter](dispute-letter-templates.md).

## After you hit send

- Expect a reply in roughly 30 (±15) days. Possible outcomes: *deleted* (best case: item removed), *corrected* (updated as asked), *verified* (bureau says the furnisher stands by it), or *deemed frivolous* (rare, and usually a sign the letter was too vague — send a more precise one).
- Log every milestone on the [tracker worksheet](worksheet-dispute-tracker.md): date mailed, certified receipt number, expected-due date, outcome.
- If an item comes back "verified" and you're sure it's wrong: send a *new* dispute that adds specific new evidence and engages the specific claim ("and here is the statement showing that"). Generic re-disputes can be declined as frivolous; documented ones are hard to wave off.
- If a deleted item later reappears (re-insertion), the bureau is generally required to notify you within about 5 business days of doing so; re-dispute and cite the deletion if it silently returns.
- Going straight to the *furnisher* is also fully legitimate — you can write the same kind of plain letter to the creditor or collections agency itself, which is often faster. There's a template for that too.

## What not to do while a dispute is open

- Don't dispute the same item at all three bureaus simultaneously with every possible complaint attached — it muddies what's being checked and can look like a template mill.
- Don't dispute *everything* in the file on principle ("dispute all negatives ASAP" is scam-territory advice). Dispute what is actually wrong.
- Don't include originals, an entire report when one row suffices, or your full un-redacted Social Security number on enclosures.
- Don't skip the certification because it costs a few dollars. It's the cheapest insurance in this whole guide.

The last mile: when the corrected (or verified) answers come back, read the *new* copy of your report the same way as [chapter 01](01-pull-your-reports-safely.md), then freeze your credit [chapter 03](03-freezes-beat-monitoring.md) so the next surprise can't be a new account in your name.
