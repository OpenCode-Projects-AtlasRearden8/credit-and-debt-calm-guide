# Collector-call scripts

*Reading time: about 9 minutes. This is chapter 06 of [The Credit and Debt Calm Guide](index.md).*

A debt collector's whole job is calling people who dread the calls. This chapter is five short scripts and enough context to use them calmly. You are allowed to be polite and firmly unhelpful at the same time; that combination is, in fact, the whole game.

## Your rights in plain words (federal baseline, by law)

Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), third-party debt collectors may not:

- Call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m., your local time.
- Keep calling your workplace after being told the employer doesn't allow it.
- Harass you, use obscene language, threaten things they can't or won't do, or imply you're a criminal.
- Tell your relatives, your roommate, or your employer about the debt (beyond one permissible call to locate you).
- Send you a validation notice promptly after first contact — and you generally have the right to dispute and request verification in writing within 30 days of that notice.
- Keep contacting you about a debt you've disputed in writing, until they provide verification.

Debts that have been sold get resold often; plenty of collectors lose the paperwork and *hope you're too embarrassed to ask for it*. Asking for it is free and legal — see script 2.

## The five ground rules (read before any script)

1. **You control the channel.** You may ask to be contacted only in writing. You may not be punished for saying that.
2. **Don't acknowledge, promise, or admit anything on a first call.** Acknowledging a debt — or making even a tiny "trial" payment to make it stop — can restart time limits in many states. Everything procedural comes *after* written verification.
3. **Get every promise in writing** — otherwise, sadly, it didn't happen.
4. **Log every call.** Date, time, number, company, what was said, the representative's name (that's what the [call log worksheet](worksheet-collector-call-log.md) is for). Collectors change their behavior noticeably when they realize notes are being kept.
5. **Verify before paying, always.** Wrong phone numbers, bought-but-mislabeled portfolios, and identity-blend errors are common in this industry. Paying a legitimate-sounding stranger who mislabeled the debt doesn't clear the real one.

## Script 1 — answering an unexpected call

*Your goal: facts, and only facts.*

> "Can you tell me who's calling and from what company? ... And can you give me a mailing address and phone number I can reach you at? ... I'm not acknowledging or denying that I owe anything on this call. Before I discuss anything, please send me written information about the debt — the amount, who it was originally owed to, and documentation. After that, I'd prefer we communicate in writing."

Then: date and log it on the worksheet. End the call; nothing else needed. A pause and a "please mail it before I say anything else" is a complete response, and you will never regret not improvising more of one.

## Script 2 — requesting written validation

*Your goal: written proof before conversation.*

> "This is a request to validate the debt you're calling about. Please mail me, in writing: the amount, the name of the original creditor, copies of documentation showing that I owe it, and any interest or fees that were added. I'm formally disputing this debt and requesting validation. Until that verification arrives, please treat this as a request to communicate only in writing."

And then the letter says it too, so paper beats memory: the [validation template](dispute-letter-templates.md) exists for exactly this. Timing matters: a **30-day written dispute window generally opens when you receive the collector's first written notice**, not on the date of a phone call. Dispute within that window and the collector must stop contacting you about the debt until they've provided verification. It's the single most under-used right in debt collection; the template file has the timing note.

## Script 3 — "this isn't mine"

A disputed debt should not be paid *to make calls stop.*

> "I dispute this debt. It doesn't belong to me — it may be identity theft or a wrong account. Please send validation to my address, and don't contact me about it again until you've provided it."

If the unfamiliar debt is *real* identity theft, this moves to identitytheft.gov and your dispute letters ([chapter 02](02-dispute-errors-plain-letters.md)) — the FTC's recovery plan is free and literally designed for the situation — so most of the heavy lifting will already be familiar.

## Script 4 — ending calls, and stops calls altogether

If calls are constant, or the tone is harassment, or they continue to your workplace after being told: request a stop in writing, not on the phone.

> "By law I'm entitled to ask that you stop contacting me. Please send a written confirmation and direct any future communications to my mailing address only."

Send that as a letter (template in the [templates chapter](dispute-letter-templates.md)), certified mail, keep the receipt. The law allows collectors to still send limited things — a court-summons notification, or a statement that they won't be contacting you further. But pattern-of-harassment talk gets routed to paper, which also protects you: a written request beside a log of unanswered calls afterward is strong evidence if you report via [CFPB complaint](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/) and your state Attorney General (both free).

## Script 5 — your relative or roommate is getting the calls

A collector may call a third party once, briefly, to try to locate you. Beyond that:

> "I don't have anything to do with this debt and can't discuss someone else's finances. The law limits collectors to one call to locate a person — please make a note that this number has told you to stop. Future calls here can be reported."

Then log the calls you're aware of. The relative can report repeat calls to the CFPB too — third parties have the same right to a harassment-free phone as you do.

## What the statute of limitations means (and the partial-payment trap)

Every state sets a time limit for *suing* over an old debt. If a collector keeps threatening a lawsuit over something old, check the actual statute for your state — your state AG's consumer site or CFPB pages list them — and know **that making a partial payment, or sometimes a written acknowledgment, can restart the clock in a number of states.** That's the origin of ground rule 2: the polite payment that stops today's call can move a six-year-old debt back to year zero. This guide deliberately doesn't quote each state's deadline — those numbers change — but your state Attorney General's consumer site keeps the up-to-date answer.

## If they break the rules

Log it precisely (the worksheet), then report — free:

- **CFPB complaint:** files into a system the collector must formally answer, gets you a case number, and usually gets a response within about a month. [consumerfinance.gov/complaint](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/)
- **Your state Attorney General** — often the fastest-moving consumer protection office, and it's also who can tell you your state's statute of limitations if you're unsure where to look.
- Keep every call's log entry and every certified-mail receipt as the file you'd hand an advocate — because a harassment case wins on *paper*, not memory.

And through everything: keep the [dispute tracker worksheet](worksheet-dispute-tracker.md) running in parallel. Collectors come and go; your own paper trail is the only record bystanders can't argue with.
